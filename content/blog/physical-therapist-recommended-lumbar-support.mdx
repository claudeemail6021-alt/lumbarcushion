Physical therapists have specific criteria for lumbar support products that differ from what most consumer marketing emphasizes. They prioritize neutral spine alignment, support consistency over time, and products that promote proper posture rather than create dependency.

Here is what physical therapists actually recommend — and why.

## What Physical Therapists Look For

**Neutral lumbar support, not overcorrection.** The goal of lumbar support is to maintain the spine's natural inward curve, not to push the back into an exaggerated arch. Cushions that are too firm or too high can overcorrect the lumbar curve and actually increase disc load. PTs look for products that support the natural curve without forcing it.

**Consistency over hours.** A cushion that works for the first hour but compresses and loses support by hour three is worse than no cushion — it creates false comfort initially and then fails. High-density foam or the clinical lumbar roll design provides more consistent support throughout a full work day.

**Appropriate firmness for the individual.** The "right" lumbar cushion varies by body size, natural curve depth, and pain type. What PTs rarely recommend is the softest, most plush option — soft cushions feel comfortable initially but provide inadequate structural support.

**The lumbar roll principle.** The McKenzie lumbar roll approach — placing a cylindrical roll at the lumbar region — has the longest clinical evidence base of any passive lumbar support. Physical therapists have been recommending this approach since the 1980s.

## What PTs Say About Lumbar Support Use

**Use it consistently, not just when you are in pain.** The value of lumbar support is in preventing the postural collapse that leads to pain, not just in treating pain once it has developed. PTs recommend using your cushion from the start of your sitting session, not waiting until your back starts to ache.

**It is not a substitute for movement.** No lumbar cushion eliminates the need for regular movement breaks. The clinical recommendation is still to stand or walk for a few minutes at least every 45–60 minutes during prolonged sitting. A cushion reduces the harm from prolonged sitting; movement eliminates it.

**Correct positioning is everything.** The most common mistake is positioning the cushion too high (at the mid-back) or too low (at the tailbone). The lumbar support should fill the inward curve of your lower back — roughly at belt-line height. When correct, you should feel gentle, even pressure. When wrong, you will feel pressure at the wrong place.

**Pair it with good chair settings.** A lumbar cushion compensates for a poorly adjusted chair. But a properly adjusted chair — seat height so your feet are flat on the floor, seat pan depth leaving 2–3 fingers of clearance behind the knees — reduces how much work the cushion needs to do.

## A Note on Lumbar Support and Core Strength

Some patients worry that using lumbar support will weaken their core muscles over time. Physical therapists generally do not share this concern for passive cushion support (as opposed to rigid back braces worn during movement).

A lumbar cushion does not restrict your movement or prevent your muscles from working — it simply provides an external reference for good posture. Used correctly, it encourages rather than replaces active lumbar muscle engagement.
