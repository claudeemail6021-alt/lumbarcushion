## Why Your Lower Back Hurts When You Sit

Lower back pain is the world's leading cause of disability, affecting an estimated 619 million people globally. And sitting — the default mode for modern work, commuting, and leisure — is a major contributing factor.

Here's why: the human spine has a natural S-shaped curve. When you sit without support, especially for extended periods, that curve tends to flatten. The lower back (lumbar region) loses its natural lordotic curve, muscles fatigue, and intervertebral discs experience uneven pressure. Over hours and years, this contributes to chronic pain, disc degeneration, and nerve compression.

A lumbar support cushion works by filling the gap between your lower back and the chair, maintaining the natural curve and reducing the muscular effort required to stay upright.

## The Anatomy of Lumbar Support

Your lumbar spine consists of five vertebrae (L1–L5) at the base of your spine, above the sacrum. The region most critical for support is typically L4–L5, where the curve is most pronounced and where the most common disc herniations occur.

Effective lumbar support should:
- Contact your back at approximately belt-line height
- Fill the space between your lower back and the chair without pushing your back into an exaggerated arch
- Be firm enough to maintain its shape under your weight, but not so rigid it creates pressure points

## Types of Lumbar Cushions

**Memory foam cushions** are the most popular. They conform to your shape, distribute pressure evenly, and maintain support over long periods. The key variable is density — low-density foam bottoms out; high-density foam (40D+) provides lasting structure.

**Lumbar rolls** are cylindrical foam cushions — the classic design recommended by physical therapists for decades. Simple, proven, and highly portable. The McKenzie lumbar roll is the gold standard.

**Inflatable cushions** pack flat for travel and allow custom firmness via inflation level. Less supportive than foam, but indispensable for air travel where foam cushions are impractical.

**Dual-density cushions** layer soft outer foam over a firm inner core — providing initial comfort while maintaining structural support over hours of use.

## How to Position Your Lumbar Cushion

Positioning is critical. A misplaced cushion can cause more harm than no cushion at all.

**Correct positioning:**
- The widest/fullest part of the curve should align with your lumbar region (roughly belt-line height, or L4–L5)
- You should feel gentle, comfortable pressure — not sharp pressure
- Your feet should be flat on the floor
- Your hips should be level with or slightly higher than your knees

**Common mistakes:**
- Placing the cushion too high (supporting mid-back instead of lumbar)
- Placing it too low (pushing on the sacrum rather than lumbar)
- Using a cushion that's too thick, pushing your spine forward rather than supporting it

## Who Benefits Most

**Office workers** who sit for 6+ hours daily benefit most dramatically. If your chair's built-in lumbar support doesn't suit your spine's specific curve, an aftermarket cushion can be transformative.

**Drivers and commuters** deal with car seats designed for the average body, not theirs. A lumbar cushion adapts the seat to your spine rather than forcing your spine to adapt to the seat.

**Frequent flyers** suffer some of the worst lumbar neglect — airplane economy seats offer almost zero lumbar support. An inflatable travel cushion is one of the best investments for anyone who flies more than once a month.

**Post-surgery or physical therapy patients** may use lumbar rolls as part of their prescribed rehabilitation — always follow your provider's specific guidance.
